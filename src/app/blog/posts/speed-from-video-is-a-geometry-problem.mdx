The [overspeed detection system](/projects/advanced-overspeed-detection-system)
was my first real computer-vision project, and it broke an assumption I'd
carried in from coursework: that the model is the hard part.

Getting YOLOv8 to draw reliable boxes around vehicles in traffic footage took
roughly an afternoon. Turning a sequence of those boxes into a number I'd be
willing to put on an enforcement notice took months. That work was geometry,
calibration, and tracking — barely any of it was machine learning.

## Pixels aren't metres, and the exchange rate keeps changing

Here's the problem in one sentence: a car moving at a constant speed covers
wildly different numbers of pixels per frame depending on where it is in the
image.

A camera looking down a road sees vehicles that are large and fast-moving in
pixel terms when near, small and slow-moving when far. Same physical speed,
completely different pixel velocity. If you naively compute *pixels moved ÷
time elapsed* and apply a single conversion factor, you get a system that
systematically overestimates speed for near vehicles and underestimates it for
distant ones.

There are two honest ways out. You can calibrate — establish the homography
between the image plane and the road plane, using known real-world distances
(lane markings are conveniently standardised) to map pixel coordinates into
ground coordinates. Or you can sidestep the mapping entirely.

I ended up leaning on the second: **dual-camera analytics**. Put two cameras a
known distance apart along the road, detect when a vehicle crosses each one, and
compute speed from the time difference. You're no longer asking "how many metres
is this pixel displacement," which is the question that requires calibration.
You're asking "when did this vehicle cross line A and line B," and the distance
between them is something you measured with a tape.

The trade-off is that you've moved the hard problem rather than removed it. Now
you need to know that the vehicle at camera B is the *same* vehicle that passed
camera A.

## Tracking is where the bodies are buried

Detection gives you boxes per frame. Speed needs trajectories. The bridge is
tracking, and tracking is where a demo that looks great on clean footage falls
apart on real roads.

Occlusion in dense traffic was the worst of these. Indian traffic is not a
sparse motorway — vehicles are close, lane discipline is loose, two-wheelers weave
between larger vehicles. A tracker tuned on highway footage degrades badly here.

The practical fix was less about a cleverer tracker and more about **being
willing to refuse to answer**. If a track was fragmented, or the confidence on
the A→B match was weak, the system emitted no reading rather than a guess. For
enforcement, a missed violation costs you very little. A fabricated one costs you
the credibility of the entire system.

## OCR was the most humbling part

Reading number plates is the step that sounds trivially easy and is not.

Off-the-shelf OCR is trained largely on documents: high contrast, flat, well-lit,
consistent fonts. A number plate in traffic footage is none of those. It's
angled, motion-blurred, sometimes dusty or partially obscured, lit by whatever
the sun and headlights are doing. Indian plates add real font variation and a
fair amount of non-standard formatting.

What actually helped:

- **Crop tight before you OCR.** Feed the plate region, not the vehicle box.
  Every extra pixel of car body is noise.
- **Choose your frame.** You get many frames of the same vehicle. Don't OCR the
  first one — pick the sharpest, largest, most frontal crop from the track.
- **Vote across frames.** Run OCR on several good crops and take the consensus
  per character. A single read is a coin flip; five reads with agreement is
  evidence.
- **Validate against the format.** Indian plates follow a known pattern. A read
  that violates it is wrong, and knowing that lets you reject or correct it.

That last point generalises: **constrain your output space wherever the domain
gives you a constraint for free.** It's the cheapest accuracy you will ever buy.

## What I'd do differently

If I restarted this today, I'd spend the first two weeks on calibration and
tracking with a frozen, off-the-shelf detector — not touching the model at all.
Every meaningful error in my final system traced back to geometry or identity,
not to detection quality. I had the instinct to improve the part I knew how to
improve, and it was the part that was already fine.

I'd also build a small ground-truth set much earlier: footage where I actually
knew the vehicle speeds. Without it, you're comparing your system's output to
your own intuition about how fast a car looks, which is not a measurement.
