Somewhere in the last twelve months the conversation about AI agents changed
tone. Less "agents will do everything," more "here is the narrow thing agents do
reliably and here is the much larger thing they don't." That's a healthier place
to be, and it's worth writing down what the shift actually consists of.

I'm writing this as someone building small systems, not running an enterprise
rollout. Take the numbers as industry context and the opinions as mine.

## The 90/10 split

The most useful heuristic I've picked up: roughly **90% workflows, 10% true
agents** in anything running in production.

A workflow is a pipeline you designed, where an LLM does specific bounded work at
specific steps. You control the sequence. An agent decides its own sequence — it
takes a goal, picks tools, and iterates until it thinks it's done.

The second is more impressive and much harder to operate. It's non-deterministic
in a way that makes debugging genuinely difficult: the same input can take a
different path today than yesterday, so "reproduce the bug" stops being a simple
instruction.

Industry data lines up with this. Gartner expects around 40% of enterprise apps
to embed task-specific agents by the end of this year, up from under 5% in
2025 — but also expects a substantial share of agentic projects to be cancelled
by 2027. Both things are true. Adoption is real; so is the pilot graveyard.

## Protocols are the genuinely new thing

If I had to name one development that changed how systems get built rather than
how they get marketed, it's the protocol layer.

**MCP** (Model Context Protocol) standardises how a model connects to data and
tools. **A2A** standardises how agents talk to each other. Before these, every
integration was bespoke plumbing — a custom adapter per tool, per project,
thrown away at the end.

The practical effect is compositional. You write an MCP server for a data source
once, and anything speaking MCP can use it. That's an unglamorous
infrastructure win, and it's the kind that tends to matter more over time than
whatever the current best model is.

The friction that's emerged: once an organisation has a dozen MCP servers,
managing them becomes its own problem. Who owns them, what they're permitted to
touch, how you audit what an agent did through them. That's governance work, and
it's arriving faster than most teams planned for.

## Multi-agent is oversold, but not by as much as I expected

The "orchestra of specialised agents" framing gets deployed a lot. The reported
reality is more mixed — surveys this year put the average enterprise at around a
dozen agents, with roughly half operating in isolation rather than coordinating
with anything.

So the architecture diagrams are ahead of the deployments. Fine — that's normal.
But I've come around on the underlying idea, for a reason that's less about
capability and more about debuggability: **a specialised agent with three tools
and a narrow brief is something you can reason about.** A generalist agent with
thirty tools is not. Decomposition helps you, the operator, more than it helps
the model.

The cost is coordination overhead, which is real and often underestimated. Every
sub-agent needs its own reliable data access, and every handoff is a place where
context gets lost.

## The part that hasn't changed

Human judgment is still load-bearing, and the data says so fairly bluntly.
Developers reportedly use AI for something like 60% of their work while being
able to fully hand off only a small fraction of tasks. The gap between "AI
touched this" and "AI owned this end to end" remains wide.

That matches my experience. The tools are excellent at the middle of a task —
generating the obvious implementation, refactoring across files, explaining an
unfamiliar codebase. They're weakest exactly where the work is hardest: deciding
what to build, noticing that the requirement is wrong, knowing which failure is
worth chasing.

## Where I've landed

The honest summary is that agents are a real capability with a narrower
applicable range than the discourse suggests, and the useful skill isn't prompting
them — it's **knowing when not to reach for one.**

That's less exciting than the 2025 version of this conversation. It's also the
first time the advice has felt actionable. I'd rather build the boring 90% well
and use genuine agency where it earns its variance.

Currently working through what this means for my own projects — particularly
whether a retrieval pipeline benefits from an agentic query-rewriting step or
just gets less predictable. I suspect the answer is "measure it," which is
usually the answer.
