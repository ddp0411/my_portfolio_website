There's a version of the Western Ghats that most people never see. From October
to May the hills around Pune are brown, dry, and honestly a bit austere — good
for a fort trek, not much to look at. Then the monsoon arrives and the same
slopes turn a green that looks digitally enhanced, waterfalls appear where there
was no water at all, and clouds sit *below* you on the ridge lines.

I'd been meaning to get to **Bhandardara** for years. This August I finally did.

## Arthur Lake and the dam

The centre of Bhandardara is **Arthur Lake**, held back by **Wilson Dam** — a
stone structure from the British era that's still doing its job over a century
on. In the dry season it's a pleasant reservoir. In August it's full, and the
whole valley reorganises itself around that fact.

The thing to time is **Umbrella Falls**. It only exists when the dam overflows —
water spilling over the wall and fanning out in a shape that earns the name. Miss
the overflow window and there's simply nothing there. We got ⟨fill: describe what

**Randha Falls**, a few kilometres away on the Pravara river, runs harder and
more reliably. In peak monsoon it's genuinely loud — the kind of sound you feel
before you're close enough to see anything.

## Kalsubai, and knowing when to turn around

**Mount Kalsubai** is the highest peak in Maharashtra at 1,646 metres, and the
trailhead at Bari village is close enough to Bhandardara to make it the obvious
headline activity.

I'll be honest about the monsoon version: it's a different trek from the dry-season
one. The route includes several metal ladder sections bolted to rock faces, and in
the rain those are slick. The trail turns into a stream in places. And the summit
view — the actual reason people climb it — is a coin flip. Cloud cover at that
altitude in August frequently means you climb for three hours to stand in white
nothing.

## What the monsoon actually feels like

The thing nobody quite conveys: it isn't a rainy day. It's **saturation**.

Everything you own is damp within an hour of arriving and stays that way. Clothes
don't dry. Your phone is a liability unless it's in a sealed bag. The temperature
sits low enough that being wet stops being refreshing and starts being cold. It
gets dark early under that much cloud.

And it's completely worth it, because the trade is a landscape that only exists
for these three months. Grass on the slopes goes a green that reads as unreal.
Every rock face has water running down it. Cloud moves through the valley at
speed, so a view opens and closes over the course of a minute — you'll be looking
at nothing, then at the whole lake, then at nothing again.

## Would I go back?

⟨fill: your honest answer⟩

For me the appeal of the monsoon Ghats isn't that they're spectacular, though
they are. It's that they're **temporary**. The same ridge I stood on in August
will be dry brown scrub by December, and the waterfall I photographed won't
exist. There's something clarifying about a landscape with an expiry date — it
makes you pay attention in a way that permanent scenery doesn't.

Next on the list is **Kaas Plateau** near Satara, which flowers for a few weeks
around late August and September and then stops. Same logic, different mechanism.
