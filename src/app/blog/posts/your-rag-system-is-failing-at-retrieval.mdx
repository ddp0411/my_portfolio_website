There's a specific way RAG systems fail that I keep running into, in my own work
and in other people's: **retrieval quality degrades, and nothing on the dashboard
moves.**

The mechanism is simple once you see it. A language model handed incomplete
context still writes a fluent, well-structured, confident answer. It reads as
grounded. If your evaluation only scores the final answer, that answer looks
fine — so a retriever that has quietly stopped surfacing the right passages
generates no alarm at all.

I learned this the hard way on [NeuroRAG](/projects/neurorag), where for weeks my
entire evaluation process was reading outputs and deciding whether they felt
correct.

Because they fail independently, **you need at least one metric per stage.**
Track only generation and retrieval regressions hide behind fluent prose. Track
only retrieval and you miss the model fabricating on top of perfectly good
context.

That's the whole argument, and it's the thing I'd want someone to take away even
if they read nothing else here.

## The metrics worth having

Scored on the retrieval span, independent of what the generator does with the
results:

- **Context precision** — of the chunks retrieved, how many were actually
  relevant? Low precision means you're burning context budget on noise.
- **Context recall** — of the chunks that *should* have been retrieved, how many
  were? This is the one that silently drifts.
- **Precision@k / Recall@k, MRR, NDCG** — the classic IR metrics. MRR is
  particularly useful when you care whether the single best passage came back
  near the top.

Scored on the generation span:

- **Faithfulness / groundedness** — is every claim in the answer supported by the
  retrieved context? This catches fabrication specifically.
- **Answer relevance** — did it answer the question that was asked, rather than a
  neighbouring one?

## Score them on the same trace

The detail that makes this practical: instrument retrieval and generation as
spans on a **single trace per query**, and attach metrics to their respective
spans. When a score drops you want to open one trace and see the query, the
retrieved chunks, the assembled prompt, and the output — in order.

Without that, "faithfulness dropped 8% this week" is a fact you can't act on. You
can't tell whether the retriever changed, the corpus changed, the chunking
changed, or the prompt changed. With per-span traces, you look at ten failing
examples and usually know within minutes.

## Build the smallest harness that works

The version of this advice that gets ignored is the one that starts with "adopt
an evaluation platform." You don't need one to begin. You need:

1. **A fixed question set.** Thirty to fifty questions covering the range of what
   users actually ask, including questions your corpus *can't* answer.
2. **Known-good source passages** for each. This is the tedious part and there's
   no shortcut — you read the corpus and mark the right answers.
3. **A script** that runs the pipeline and reports retrieval hit rate at k, plus
   whatever generation check you can afford.
4. **A rule that you run it before every change you'd describe as an
   improvement.**

That's an afternoon of work and it converts "I think this helped" into "this
moved recall@5 from 0.62 to 0.71." Everything more sophisticated is an upgrade
from there, not a prerequisite.

## Those unanswerable questions matter more than you'd think

Point 1 above deserves emphasis. Include questions your corpus genuinely cannot
answer, and check that the system declines rather than improvising.

This is the single most useful test I run. A pipeline that answers everything is
not a pipeline with great recall — it's a pipeline that has never learned to say
no. For anything in a clinical, legal, or financial setting, a confident wrong
answer is meaningfully worse than a refusal, and you only find out whether you
have that property by explicitly testing for it.

## Where this is heading

The framing that's taken hold over the past year — and I think it's the right one
— is that retrieval is one part of a broader **context engineering** problem:
what goes into the model's window, in what order, compressed how, carried across
how many turns. Prompt phrasing matters much less than which three chunks the
retriever surfaced.

Graph-augmented retrieval is the interesting frontier here. Instead of returning
isolated chunks, you return connected subgraphs — entities plus their
relationships — which addresses the exact problem I hit with ICD-10, where a
diagnosis and its exclusion criteria are related but not adjacent.

I haven't rebuilt NeuroRAG on that model yet. When I do, I'll have a harness to
tell me whether it actually helped — which is more than I could say the first
time around.
